Is our own scraping lawful?

Every host the regulatory tracker fetches, checked against its robots.txt, its terms of use and its copyright policy, and analysed under Indian law.

Reviewed 25 Aug 2026 17 hosts examined 2 stopped 5 paused for decision Now fetching 12
Bottom line

Two hosts had to stop today; the rest are defensible

On fifteen of seventeen hosts there is no robots rule covering the paths we fetch and no clause anywhere restricting automated access. The residual exposure on those is not access but reproduction, and it lands almost entirely on the optional PDF archive rather than on the dashboard, which stores bibliographic facts that attract no copyright at all.

Two hosts are different in kind, and I have stopped the tracker on both rather than leave them running while you decide. On these the objection is to the collection itself, not to what we do with the result.

uidai.gov.in — excludes every crawler

User-Agent: *
Disallow: /

That is the entire file. It is the strongest exclusion the protocol can express, it admits no exception, and it does not vary by user agent — I verified both. Sending a browser string would not take us outside it; it would make unattended collection look like concealment. UIDAI's copyright policy separately permits reproduction “for research or private study but not for sale or for use in conjunction with commercial purposes”, which is the only clause in the survey that reaches a commercial firm directly.

eservices.dot.gov.in — names web scraping as unauthorised

“Unauthorised activities: The term Unauthorised activities includes any activity which is punishable under section 43 (for eg web scraping, altering source code, hacking, introducing viruses etc.) and Section 45 of the Information Technology Act, 2000…”

The same agreement adds that the portal “shall not in any manner be used for any unauthorized activity” and that services “shall be accessed only through the interfaces expressly authorised by DoT”. It names the exact activity on the exact host, and it is not qualified by volume, harm or purpose, so none of our proportionality facts take us outside its literal words. The permissive copyright policy on the same site does not cure this: that clause licenses reuse, this one restricts the method of collection.

This is the costly one. DoT circulars and the Act-and-Rules shelf were core telecom coverage. The remedy is in the agreement itself, which contemplates “activity authorised in writing by DoT”: a letter seeking written authorisation is the route back, and worth sending this week.

Paused, not stopped

Three sites consent in their crawl policy but block identified agents

MeitY publishes User-agent: * / Allow: /, which expressly permits crawling of every path, while its edge returns 403 to any user agent that identifies itself as a bot. Only an exact consumer-browser string is served. PIB behaves the same way. So the site consents in the document written for crawlers and refuses in the layer that enforces it.

Reaching either means presenting a browser string, which is precisely the practice this review otherwise recommends against. That is a judgment call about the firm's own posture rather than a technical question, so I have paused the four MeitY sources and PIB for your decision instead of resolving it silently in either direction. The coverage cost is smaller than it looks: gazetted MeitY instruments are still reachable through the e-Gazette lane, and MeitY's own gazette listing has not moved since 16 December 2025.

What changed in the code

Compliance is now enforced, not promised

Already done
  • robots.txt is a hard pre-flight gate. Every request is checked against the origin's own robots.txt before it is made, evaluated under RFC 9309. A disallowed path is refused by the engine regardless of what the registry says.
  • The spoofed Chrome user agent is gone. The tracker now identifies itself as TMT-Regulatory-Radar/2.0 with a contact mailbox. Set TMT_RADAR_CONTACT to a real monitored address before the next run.
  • Suspensions are visible, not hidden. The eight stopped and five paused sources appear on the coverage page with their reason, so the tracker never overstates what it is watching.
  • Every request is attributed and logged to the source that caused it, with a per-run provenance record: this run made 40 requests across 12 hosts, none to an undeclared host.

Writing that gate immediately proved its worth, and in an instructive way. My first implementation used Python's standard robots parser and wrongly blocked all of MIB and ASCI: MIB's robots.txt itself returns 403, which the legacy parser treats as a blanket deny although RFC 9309 treats an unavailable file as no restriction, and ASCI's disallow lines sit above any User-agent line, so they bind nobody — its only real group is Disallow:, meaning allow everything. Both were caught by testing the gate against the live sites rather than trusting it.

It is the one host where losing metadata-only discipline would create real and unmitigated exposure.

The legal footing

Why the dashboard is near-unassailable and the archive needs discipline

The instinct that government material is simply free is wrong. Under s.2(k) read with s.17(d) of the Copyright Act 1957, the Government is first owner of copyright in government works. What actually protects us is narrower and more specific.

  • The dashboard stores facts, not expression. Copyright subsists only in original works, and in Eastern Book Company v. D.B. Modak the Supreme Court rejected sweat-of-the-brow in favour of skill and judgment. A title, a date, a document type and a URL are bibliographic facts. This is the strongest part of our position.
  • s.52(1)(q)(i) permits reproduction of matter published in an Official Gazette, except an Act. It should not be overclaimed: it covers the gazetted instrument, not every page of a regulator's website. s.52(1)(q)(ii) is a trap that cuts against us — an Act may be reproduced only together with commentary or other original matter, so archiving a clean PDF of an Act falls outside it.
  • s.52(1)(a) fair dealing is the operative safe harbour for the archive, and the 2012 Explanation expressly covers electronic storage for those purposes. The position improved materially last month: in ANI Media v. OpenAI (Delhi HC, 24 July 2026) the court held prima facie that no non-commercial limitation can be read into s.52(1)(a), that “private” extends to a closed corporate group, and approved the Canadian CCH holding that lawyers researching to advise clients for profit are engaged in research. That is this use case. It is, however, an interim ruling of a single judge refusing an injunction.
  • IT Act s.43 and s.66. s.43 turns on access “without permission”, which is exactly why robots.txt matters evidentially even though it is not law. s.66 additionally requires dishonesty or fraud, and an openly identified crawler running at one request per second and honouring exclusions is the opposite of that.
  • There is no text-and-data-mining exception in Indian law, unlike the EU, Singapore or Japan. We cannot lean on one.

Two questions here are genuinely unsettled and should not be reported upward as clean: whether fetching a page served openly at HTTP 200 is “without permission” for s.43, and whether browsewrap terms bind a party who never assented to them.

What to do

Recommended next steps

  1. Decide on MeitY and PIB. Either accept a browser user agent for those two hosts on the footing that their robots.txt expressly permits crawling, and record that reasoning in a file note, or leave them paused and rely on the e-Gazette lane.
  2. Write to DoT seeking written authorisation for the Telecom eServices Portal, which its own user agreement contemplates. This restores the most valuable suspended source.
  3. Set a real contact mailbox in TMT_RADAR_CONTACT, monitored by someone who would notice a request to stop.
  4. Keep the PDF archive off by default for the eight hosts with restrictive reproduction terms, and send the one-line permission emails where the archive is actually wanted.
  5. Keep the archive internal. No circulation to clients, no external distribution, no outward-facing work product without separate clearance.
  6. Never frame these pages. TRAI, MTCTE, MeitY, CERT-In, UIDAI, IN-SPACe and PRGI all expressly prohibit loading their pages into frames. Link out, always.
  7. Record a dated file note of the firm's considered position, so that if anyone ever asks, the answer is a contemporaneous document rather than a reconstruction.
Limits of this review

What this does not establish

  • This is a compliance review of publicly stated terms observed on 25 August 2026. It is not a legal opinion, it has not been settled by counsel, and you are the lawyers.
  • Terms change without notice, and several of these hosts are demonstrably unstable. The review should be repeated periodically.
  • One real gap: MIB's separate Terms and Conditions tab could not be retrieved — its AJAX endpoints returned empty shells and a 500. MIB's clean position on automated access is therefore provisional until that tab is read.
  • Documents were not classified individually against s.52(1)(q). Whether a given instrument is gazette matter, an Act, or an order of a judicial authority is a document-by-document question. In particular the TDSAT path we fetch is an administrative notices listing rather than judgments, and a CBFC order is an administrative order of a statutory board, so neither is automatically within limb (iv).
  • Absence of a restriction is not permission. On TDSAT, IN-SPACe and the e-Gazette the finding is silence, which supports an implied-permission argument but is not a grant.
